# The Camera Does Not Carry a Gun

## Surveillance and the State’s Monopoly on Violence

A roadside license-plate camera does not look threatening. It does not wear a uniform, display a badge, carry handcuffs, issue commands, or place anyone under arrest. It simply stands beside the road, silently photographing passing vehicles and transmitting what it observes into a database.

That apparent passivity is precisely what makes the modern surveillance state so easy to misunderstand.

When most people see a camera, they think about observation. They compare it to a security camera outside a store, a doorbell camera on a neighbor’s home, or a police officer seeing a license plate while driving through town. From that perspective, an automated license-plate reader may seem like little more than an efficient pair of eyes.

But a government surveillance camera is not merely a pair of eyes. It is an extension of an institution possessing powers no private citizen, corporation, neighborhood association, church, or civic organization can lawfully claim. It supplies information to an institution that can stop people, detain them, search them, seize their property, arrest them, imprison them, and use escalating physical force against them if they resist.

The camera does not carry a gun.

It reports to those who do.

That is the relationship we must understand if we are to understand the surveillance state.

## What Is the Monopoly on Violence?

The phrase “monopoly on violence” often appears in political philosophy, libertarian writing, anarchist theory, sociology, and debates over the nature of government. To people outside those circles, it may sound abstract, academic, or deliberately provocative.

The idea itself is straightforward.

Within a defined territory, the state claims a unique authority to determine when physical force may lawfully be used. It authorizes police officers to detain and arrest. It authorizes courts to impose punishment. It authorizes officials to seize property. It establishes the conditions under which its agents may use force, including deadly force.

Individuals may sometimes use force in self-defense, but they do so under rules ultimately interpreted and enforced by the state. A private citizen cannot create a police department, declare jurisdiction over the town, compel obedience, levy taxes, operate a jail, and issue final judgments in disputes involving himself.

The state claims that authority for itself.

Murray Rothbard described the state as “that organization in society which attempts to maintain a monopoly of the use of force and violence in a given territorial area.” He emphasized that other institutions ordinarily acquire their resources through voluntary exchange, while the state ultimately relies upon coercion.[^rothbard-anatomy]

Ludwig von Mises described the state as “the social apparatus of coercion and compulsion.”[^mises-market] Elsewhere, he wrote that government “has the monopoly of violent action.”[^mises-society] The defining characteristic of government is not that it employs people, owns buildings, maintains records, or purchases technology. Private institutions do all those things. Its defining characteristic is that its commands can be backed by legally authorized force.

A church may ask for a contribution. A business may offer a service. A charity may request assistance. A social organization may expel a member. A newspaper may criticize someone.

Government can issue a command.

Behind that command stands enforcement. Behind enforcement stands physical restraint. Behind resistance stands the possibility of escalating violence.

This does not mean every government action is wicked or that every government employee is malicious. It means that government possesses a relationship to force that no other institution can claim. That unique power demands unique restraint.

## Why Government Surveillance Is Different

A private store may record a vehicle entering its parking lot. It may use the recording to investigate theft, enforce rules on its property, or determine whether a particular person entered the premises.

But the store ordinarily cannot follow the driver onto a public road, activate emergency lights, compel the vehicle to stop, order the occupants to show their hands, demand identification, search the vehicle, confiscate property, place the driver in handcuffs, or transport that person to a detention facility.

The police can.

A neighbor may see that you left home at eight in the morning. A coworker may notice your car outside a restaurant. A gas station employee may see you purchase fuel. A friend may recognize your vehicle passing through an intersection. Those observations are limited, dispersed, temporary, and generally disconnected from one another.

A government surveillance network can collect those observations automatically, consolidate them, preserve them, search them, compare them, and use them to reconstruct a person’s movements.

The distinction is not merely one of scale, although the scale is significant. It is the union of information and coercive authority.

Surveillance conducted by the institution possessing the monopoly on violence is categorically different because the information can initiate a compelled encounter:

**Camera. Database. Search. Police encounter. Command. Force.**

The force may never be used. Most encounters may end peacefully. Most officers may behave professionally. Most searches may produce nothing dramatic. But coercive authority is always present.

When an officer activates emergency lights behind a vehicle, the driver is not receiving a friendly request that may be ignored without consequence. When an officer orders someone out of a car, the person does not negotiate as an equal party in a voluntary exchange. When an officer places someone under arrest, the individual cannot decline the arrangement and leave.

The encounter is governed by compulsion.

That is why surveillance information in police hands must be treated differently from ordinary observation.

## The Invisible Violence Behind the Camera

The physical design of modern surveillance systems conceals this relationship because the coercion does not occur at the point of collection. No one is tackled beneath the pole. No one is handcuffed by the camera. No weapon emerges from the equipment box. The device records a plate, attaches a time and location, and transmits the information somewhere else.

The consequences occur later. They may arise several miles away when an officer receives an alert, days later when an investigator searches a plate number, or months later when stored observations are used to construct a pattern of movement or association.

This separation between observation and enforcement gives the system an appearance of neutrality. People see the camera but not the command it may generate. They see the database but not the traffic stop. They see the image of a plate but not the frightened driver being ordered to keep both hands visible. They see the promise of efficiency but not the consequences of an error.

The surveillance apparatus and the coercive apparatus are presented as separate things. In practice, the surveillance apparatus exists to serve the coercive apparatus.

Government does not purchase automated license-plate readers because it is merely curious about traffic. It purchases them because the information can be acted upon. The system helps officials decide whom to investigate, whom to locate, whom to follow, whom to question, and whom to stop.

A surveillance camera does not need to exercise force directly to become part of the machinery of force. It needs only to provide actionable intelligence to those authorized to exercise it.

## The Error Is Not Merely an Error

Every information system makes mistakes. Data may be incomplete, records may be outdated, plate numbers may be misread, vehicles may have been sold, a driver may be using a borrowed car, a plate may have been stolen or duplicated, or an officer may enter the wrong number.

An error in an advertising system may produce an irrelevant advertisement. An error in a reservation system may cause inconvenience. An error in a retail database may require a customer-service call.

An error in a police surveillance system may initiate an armed encounter.

Flashing lights appear in the rearview mirror. The driver is ordered to stop. Officers may approach while assuming that the vehicle or its occupant is dangerous. Commands may be shouted. Hands may be placed on weapons. Occupants may be ordered out, restrained, questioned, or searched.

Supporters may respond that officers are trained to investigate alerts rather than treat them as conclusive proof. That distinction is important, but it does not eliminate the coercive reality. The person stopped because of a false alert must still submit to the encounter.

The government’s mistake becomes the citizen’s emergency.

The greater the power attached to a database, the more demanding our standards for its use must become. A system capable of producing detention, search, seizure, or armed confrontation cannot be evaluated merely by asking whether it is convenient or whether it occasionally produces useful information.

We must ask what happens when it is wrong, who bears the consequences, and how much innocent activity is recorded in order to identify the comparatively small amount of activity government considers suspicious.

Most importantly, we must ask why an institution possessing the power to compel obedience should be permitted to collect broad information about people who are not suspected of any crime.

## From Particularized Suspicion to Population Surveillance

Traditional law enforcement was theoretically organized around particularized suspicion. A crime occurred. Evidence was gathered. A suspect was identified. Investigators then sought additional information related to the specific offense.

The modern surveillance state reverses that sequence. It collects information about everyone first.

Government may not yet know what crime it is investigating. It may have no suspect and no individualized grounds to believe that a particular person has done anything wrong. Nevertheless, the system records that person’s movements and preserves them for possible future use.

Every passing vehicle becomes an entry. Every driver becomes searchable. Every journey becomes potentially relevant to an investigation that may not yet exist.

The justification is that the overwhelming majority of innocent records may someday help locate a guilty person. That is an extraordinary inversion of the relationship between the individual and the state.

Instead of government demonstrating a reason to investigate someone before collecting information, government collects information so that it may later decide whom to investigate.

This is not merely a more efficient version of an officer seeing a plate while patrolling. Human observation is limited by time, place, memory, attention, and purpose. A patrol officer sees what happens to pass before him. He does not possess perfect recall of every vehicle, every location, every time, and every association.

Automated surveillance removes those natural limits. The system sees continuously, remembers automatically, searches retrospectively, and connects observations across time and place. It permits government to ask not only, “Where is the vehicle we are looking for?” but also, “Where has this vehicle been?” “Which vehicles appeared near it?” “How often did it visit this location?” and “What pattern can be inferred from its movements?”

The result is not simply observation.

It is institutional memory backed by coercive authority.

## Power Must Be Narrowest Where Its Consequences Are Greatest

Defenses of government power often contain an unspoken premise: the people entrusted with the power will be virtuous.

The officer will be virtuous. The police chief will be virtuous. The prosecutor, administrator, auditor, council member, judge, and future officeholder will all use the authority only for its intended purpose. They will resist curiosity, convenience, pressure, anger, ambition, political fashion, and personal interest.

This assumption is not merely optimistic. It contradicts what we know about human beings.

Government agents are not drawn from a separate and more virtuous species. They are people. They possess loyalties, ambitions, prejudices, fears, frustrations, blind spots, career incentives, personal relationships, and private interests just as everyone else does.

Some are honorable. Some are not. Most are mixtures of good and bad motives, as human beings ordinarily are.

The decisive difference is that government agents possess something the rest of us do not: access to the state’s monopoly on violence.

A private person’s selfishness may cause an insult, a broken promise, a dishonest transaction, or a personal betrayal. A government agent’s selfishness, error, impatience, or prejudice can be joined to detention, seizure, prosecution, and force.

This is why the consequences of official conduct are so much greater. It is also why official power must be narrower rather than broader.

A free society cannot be designed around the expectation of universal virtue. It must be designed in recognition of ordinary human nature. Its safeguards must continue to operate when officials are impatient, self-interested, mistaken, politically motivated, or simply unexceptional.

The argument for limitation does not require us to believe that every officer will abuse every power. It requires only the recognition that no institution can guarantee the permanent virtue of everyone who will control that power in the future.

A virtuous police chief can retire. A conscientious administrator can be replaced. A careful council can lose an election. A good officer can leave the department. A principled prosecutor can be succeeded by an ambitious one.

Rights cannot depend on the continuing virtue of temporary officeholders.

Power must be judged not only by the character of the person holding it today, but by the capabilities it creates for every person who may hold it tomorrow.

Surveillance architecture outlives the promises used to introduce it. Administrations, police chiefs, prosecutors, council members, and public attitudes change. Technology improves. Databases merge. Retention periods expand. Search tools become more sophisticated. Agencies create new sharing agreements.

The hardware does not care who controls it.

The database does not remember the reassuring speech delivered when it was approved.

The system simply waits for the next person authorized to search it.

## “Only for Serious Crimes”

The surveillance state rarely presents itself as a surveillance state. It presents itself through exceptional cases: a stolen vehicle, a kidnapped child, a violent fugitive, a murder suspect, or a person in immediate danger.

These examples are powerful because the harms are real. People understandably want police to recover stolen property, rescue abducted children, and apprehend violent offenders. But the emotional force of an exceptional case cannot answer the institutional question.

The question is not whether information can ever help solve a serious crime. Of course it can. The question is whether helping in some serious cases justifies building a system that records everyone.

There is an enormous difference between obtaining targeted information during a specific emergency and continuously constructing a searchable archive of innocent movement.

The assurance that surveillance will be used “only for serious crimes” also misunderstands how human beings act within institutions.

Praxeology begins with the recognition that human beings act purposefully. They choose means they believe will move them from a less satisfactory condition to a more satisfactory one. Officials are no exception. They make choices according to their goals, available options, perceived costs, expected benefits, time constraints, and personal preferences.

When the cost of using a surveillance system is high, an official has reason to reserve it for important cases. A warrant application, written justification, supervisory approval, external review, and the possibility of meaningful consequences all increase the cost of a search.

When the cost is negligible, the calculation changes.

If searching a person’s movements requires only entering a plate number into a convenient interface, if approval is routine or nonexistent, if audits are rare, and if misuse carries little chance of detection or punishment, officials will predictably use the easiest available means more often.

That does not require uniquely corrupt people. It requires ordinary people responding to incentives.

An investigator under pressure to close a case will use the available tool. An officer trying to resolve uncertainty quickly will choose the fastest source of information. A supervisor concerned with performance statistics may encourage efficient searches. An employee who is curious about a neighbor, acquaintance, former romantic partner, critic, or local political figure may decide that the risk of being caught is remote.

People tend to prefer sooner satisfaction to later satisfaction. When the immediate benefit of a search is available now and the possible cost is distant, uncertain, or trivial, the temptation toward expedient use becomes stronger. Institutional rules written on paper must compete with immediate convenience.

This is why “only for serious crimes” cannot remain merely an announced intention. Unless the limitation is built into the structure of access and backed by real costs, it will erode.

A policy that says “serious crimes only” while allowing easy, unreviewed searches is not a meaningful restraint. It is an invitation to reinterpret “serious,” expand exceptions, normalize use, and prioritize the immediate needs of the official over the distant privacy interests of the citizen.

The first exception establishes the capability. The next exception expands the category. Repeated use creates habit. Habit becomes standard procedure. Standard procedure becomes institutional expectation.

The appetite of monopoly begins with the convenience of the individual actor.

## The Appetite of Monopoly

Rothbard warned against assuming that a compulsory monopoly of violence would remain confined to the limited purposes assigned to it. State officials face incentives to expand authority, jurisdiction, resources, and discretion rather than act as perfectly neutral custodians of narrowly delegated power.[^rothbard-quotes]

This does not always occur through a grand plan. More often, it occurs through countless individual actions that appear reasonable in isolation.

Police want more effective investigative tools. Prosecutors want stronger evidence. Emergency managers want real-time awareness. Transportation officials want traffic data. Tax authorities want compliance information. Regulators want enforcement records. Intelligence agencies want patterns. Administrators want metrics.

Each request can sound modest when considered alone.

The danger emerges from accumulation.

A camera installed for one purpose becomes part of a network. The network produces a database. The database acquires search tools. Search tools produce analytical capabilities. Those capabilities attract new users. New users request additional data, broader retention, and additional reasons for access.

At each stage, the immediate actor has an incentive to expand the system. Greater access reduces the effort required to perform his task. More data reduces uncertainty. Fewer restrictions save time. Broader sharing increases convenience.

The privacy cost is dispersed across thousands of people and may not become visible for years. The institutional benefit is immediate and concentrated in the hands of the person requesting the expansion.

That imbalance makes expansion predictable.

No official needs to announce the construction of a total surveillance state. Each participant needs only to request one additional capability that makes his own work easier. The final system can become sweeping even when every individual expansion was presented as narrow and practical.

Government will nearly always desire more information because information makes coercive administration easier. It assists in locating, categorizing, investigating, regulating, taxing, prosecuting, and controlling people.

But the convenience of those who exercise power is not the highest political good.

A free society should deliberately preserve areas of uncertainty for the state. Government should not know everything it could find useful. Police should not automatically possess a historical record of every innocent journey merely because that record might occasionally make an investigation easier.

The state’s frustration with limits is often evidence that the limits are functioning. When officials complain that individualized suspicion takes too long, judicial review is inconvenient, public debate delays deployment, or privacy restrictions prevent useful searches, they are describing constitutional friction.

That friction is not necessarily a defect. It is one of the mechanisms by which a free people prevents administrative convenience from becoming unlimited authority.

## The Apparatus of Coercion

Henry Hazlitt described government and its police forces as an “apparatus of coercion.” His warning concerned the recurring temptation to use that apparatus to impose desired outcomes, but the insight applies directly to surveillance.[^hazlitt]

Once the apparatus exists, people repeatedly discover new reasons to use it. Each person imagines the machinery being directed toward a target he considers dangerous, unlawful, immoral, or socially harmful. Because he approves of the immediate objective, he becomes less cautious about the power being created.

This is where the issue of illegal immigration becomes especially important.

Many people who distrust government surveillance in other contexts become willing to accept it when the immediate target is someone believed to be in the country unlawfully. They see the enforcement goal as legitimate and therefore treat the surveillance mechanism as justified.

That reaction is understandable, but it confuses the desirability of a particular enforcement outcome with the desirability of the power used to obtain it.

A camera capable of identifying and reconstructing the movements of an undocumented immigrant is equally capable of identifying and reconstructing the movements of a gun owner, church member, political organizer, business owner, protester, journalist, government critic, medical patient, or ordinary family.

The machine does not understand why it was installed. It has no loyalty to the people who supported its deployment. It does not distinguish between a purpose one person considers legitimate and a purpose that same person would consider abusive. It records plates, locations, times, and patterns according to the permissions granted to whoever controls it.

A person may support surveillance when it is used to enforce immigration law. That same person may strongly oppose its use to identify vehicles visiting gun stores, churches, political meetings, or private homes.

Another person may support the system when it is used against violent suspects while opposing its use to identify vehicles visiting medical clinics, protests, labor meetings, advocacy organizations, or religious gatherings.

The contradiction does not lie in holding different moral judgments about those activities. The activities are not necessarily equivalent.

The contradiction lies in believing that the surveillance power will remain attached only to the purpose one approves.

It will not.

The apparatus is not limited by the political justification that secured its creation. Once installed, it becomes a general capability awaiting new rules, new users, new databases, and new definitions of acceptable use.

The system created to locate one disfavored class of people can later be redirected toward another. The legal rationale can change. The list of searchable offenses can expand. A new administration can reinterpret policy. A crisis can produce broader authority. A temporary exception can become routine practice.

The underlying error is always the same: people evaluate coercive machinery according to its present target rather than its permanent capability.

They ask, “Do I want the government to find these people?”

They do not ask, “Do I want the government to possess the power to find anyone in this manner?”

That is the question that must control the debate.

The temptation is always to support coercive machinery when it is aimed at someone else. One person accepts it for immigration enforcement. Another accepts it for firearms enforcement. Another accepts it for political extremism, narcotics, unpaid fines, vehicle repossessions, code violations, or minor warrants.

Each justification appears limited when considered alone. Together they construct a surveillance system that can eventually be used against nearly everyone.

The proper test of a government power is not whether we approve of its first target. It is whether we are prepared to entrust that power to officials we distrust, acting for purposes we oppose, against people we believe are innocent.

If the answer is no, then the power itself is the problem.

The apparatus is indifferent to ideology, motive, and allegiance.

It serves whoever controls the credentials.

## Bastiat and the Purpose of Collective Force

Frédéric Bastiat began *The Law* with a simple moral foundation. Each person possesses life, liberty, and property and may defend those rights. People may therefore organize collective force for the common defense of those same rights.

But collective force cannot legitimately acquire moral authority that no individual possesses.

The law is perverted when it is used to violate the rights it was established to protect. Bastiat warned that law can convert plunder into a right and lawful defense into a crime.[^bastiat]

This principle exposes the moral problem with population-level surveillance. An ordinary person would have no right to install equipment throughout town for the purpose of cataloging everyone’s movements, retaining those records, analyzing associations, and making the results available for undisclosed future investigations.

He could not justify the practice merely by saying that some of the people recorded might someday commit crimes. He could not demand that everyone accept constant tracking because the system might occasionally help recover stolen property. He could not claim authority to stop and interrogate anyone whose movements triggered his private database.

The moral concern does not disappear when the activity is conducted under color of law. It becomes more serious because collective surveillance is attached to collective force.

Government claims that it records innocent people in order to protect their rights. But protection becomes difficult to distinguish from domination when the protective institution demands a comprehensive account of the protected population’s movements.

Police power should be defensive and particular. It should respond to identifiable offenses, victims, evidence, and suspects. It should not become a generalized power to know where everyone has been simply because such knowledge may prove useful later.

## Hoppe and the Problem of the Final Judge

Hans-Hermann Hoppe identifies another defining feature of state power: the state claims to be the ultimate judge within its territory. It does not merely enforce rules. It is also the final institutional authority in disputes over those rules, including many disputes involving its own actions.

This creates an obvious problem for surveillance oversight.

Government purchases the system. Government writes the access policies. Government employees conduct the searches. Government supervisors review employee conduct. Government lawyers interpret disclosure obligations. Government prosecutors decide whether evidence will be used. Government courts frequently determine whether government collection was lawful. Government officials decide what violations merit discipline.

Internal audits may be useful. Written policies may be better than no policies. Public-records requirements may expose misconduct. Courts may sometimes impose meaningful limits. But none of these measures changes the underlying structure.

The institution operating the surveillance system remains deeply involved in judging the legitimacy of its own use of that system.

This is why “trust us” is not an adequate civil-liberties safeguard. Trust may be personally deserved by particular officials, but it is not an institutional limitation.

A trustworthy police chief can retire. A conscientious administrator can be replaced. An internal rule can be rewritten. A disciplined officer can be succeeded by a careless one.

Rights cannot depend on the permanent virtue of temporary officeholders.

The public needs structural limits that remain effective when virtue is absent and trust is no longer justified.

## Regulation and the Illusion of Permanent Restraint

When surveillance systems become controversial, officials often propose regulation rather than prohibition. They promise limited retention periods, logged searches, access controls, audits, disciplinary consequences, and restrictions to approved law-enforcement purposes.

These measures may reduce some forms of abuse, but they do not resolve the central problem. The state still possesses the capability, and every formal restraint remains vulnerable to the same incentives that encouraged the system’s expansion.

A retention period can be extended. An approved-purpose list can be broadened. An audit can become superficial. A sharing agreement can move information beyond the jurisdiction that collected it. A disciplinary policy can be weakly enforced. A search log may reveal misuse only after a person has already been watched.

Regulation asks government to restrain itself while preserving the power that requires restraint.

Sometimes regulation is unavoidable. Certain government functions necessarily involve sensitive information and coercive authority. In those circumstances, strict procedures, warrants, minimization, disclosure, adversarial review, and meaningful penalties may be essential.

But not every possible government capability must be preserved merely so it can be regulated. There is a difference between regulating an indispensable function and constructing a new surveillance power because technology has made it affordable.

The burden should fall upon the state to prove not merely that a system is useful, but that the function cannot be performed through narrower means. Can police use targeted alerts tied to specifically identified vehicles? Can they seek judicial authorization during an investigation? Can they rely on witness statements, ordinary patrol work, dispatch information, reported sightings, and footage from locations relevant to a known crime? Can emergency systems be activated temporarily for an identified threat rather than permanently recording the population?

The question should never be merely, “Could police find this useful?”

Almost any information could be useful.

The proper question is, “Does that usefulness justify granting this capability to the institution possessing the monopoly on violence?”

That is a much higher standard.

It should be.

## The Freedom to Move Without Creating a Government File

Freedom of movement means more than the absence of a checkpoint.

A person may technically remain free to drive while every journey is recorded, retained, and made searchable by government. No officer physically prevents the trip, and no official permission is required before departure. Yet something fundamental has changed.

The citizen travels beneath an institutional gaze. Government may later reconstruct the journey, interpret the destination, examine the frequency of visits, treat the presence of another vehicle as evidence of association, or reconsider the movement in light of an investigation that did not exist when the trip occurred.

A society does not need to prohibit movement in order to chill it. It need only make people aware that their movements may become part of a permanent official record.

People behave differently when they know they are being cataloged. They may avoid controversial gatherings, hesitate to visit politically unpopular organizations, or decline to attend protests, religious services, support groups, or private meetings. They may fear that an innocent pattern will be misunderstood.

They begin asking not whether an activity is lawful, but whether it will look suspicious to an unseen analyst in the future.

That is not the mentality of a free people.

Liberty requires room in which lawful life is lived without advance documentation for possible state inspection. Government should investigate crimes. It should not maintain a ready-made history of everyone in case a reason to investigate them later appears.

## “I Have Nothing to Hide"

The most common defense of surveillance may also be the most shortsighted:

“I have nothing to hide.”

Its companion is equally familiar:

“Don’t commit crimes and you have nothing to worry about.”

Both statements rest on an assumption that is rarely examined. The speaker assumes that the legal and political conditions under which he currently lives will remain substantially unchanged after the surveillance infrastructure is in place.

He has nothing to hide yet.

He is committing no crime yet.

That does not mean he secretly intends wrongdoing. It means that he is treating today’s laws, today’s freedoms, today’s enforcement priorities, and today’s political authorities as permanent.

They are not.

Laws change. Definitions change. Administrative rules change. Activities once tolerated become regulated. Conduct once lawful becomes prohibited. Exceptions narrow. Licenses become mandatory. Political movements gain and lose power. Emergencies are declared. New categories of suspicious behavior are created. Information collected for one purpose becomes useful for another.

The surveillance system remains.

A person who says, “I have nothing to hide,” imagines the government looking only for conduct he already considers criminal. He does not imagine a future government examining conduct he presently regards as ordinary, private, lawful, or protected.

He may support the right to own firearms, attend a particular church, educate his children at home, operate a cash business, protest government policy, assist undocumented people, receive controversial medical treatment, travel across state lines, donate to an unpopular organization, or meet privately with political allies.

Today, he may consider those activities entirely legitimate.

Tomorrow’s officials may classify some of them differently.

The critical mistake is believing that surveillance merely enforces a fixed body of law. In reality, surveillance creates a durable capability that can be redirected whenever the law, policy, or political definition of wrongdoing changes.

A database does not know which freedoms its original supporters expected to retain. It does not distinguish between a liberty and an offense. It records the same plate, time, location, and association either way.

Only the government’s current rules determine how that information is interpreted.

Those rules can change without replacing a single camera.

This is why “Don’t commit crimes” is not an adequate answer. It assumes that criminality is a permanent moral category rather than a legal classification written and revised by human institutions. It ignores the long history of governments criminalizing conduct that later generations recognize as peaceful, honorable, or necessary.

People have been punished for forbidden speech, unauthorized assembly, religious practice, interracial relationships, labor organizing, possession of prohibited literature, assisting fugitives, violating segregation laws, and resisting conscription. The fact that conduct has been declared illegal does not establish that the law is just, and obedience to every existing law is not the definition of liberty.

The danger of comprehensive surveillance is not confined to people who intend to violate good laws. It extends to people who may one day refuse to obey bad ones.

It also extends to people who remain entirely lawful but become politically inconvenient. Surveillance need not prove a crime to exert power. It can identify associations, expose private relationships, reveal attendance at meetings, generate investigative leads, produce suspicion, or chill lawful conduct.

The person who says he has nothing to hide is usually imagining his present self before a government he does not presently fear.

He should instead imagine his future self before a government he does not trust.

Would he still be comfortable with a searchable record of his movements if the officials controlling it opposed his beliefs? Would he accept the system if its definitions of extremism, public safety, contraband, misinformation, unlawful assembly, or prohibited association were written by people hostile to everything he values?

If not, then his confidence was never based on principle.

It was based on temporary political comfort.

Privacy is not the refuge of criminals. It is the space in which lawful people retain the freedom to live, associate, worship, speak, organize, dissent, and change their minds without creating a permanent government record.

The question is not whether you have something to hide from the government you have today.

The question is whether you are willing to give every government you may have tomorrow the ability to search the life you are living now.

That is a far more dangerous bargain.

## The Burden Belongs to Power

Surveillance debates are often framed as if citizens must prove that the technology will be abused.

Show us the misconduct. Identify the victim. Prove that officials searched without justification. Demonstrate that the data has already been used politically.

That reverses the proper burden.

The public should not have to wait for abuse before questioning a sweeping power. The institution seeking the authority should carry the burden of justification.

Government should have to demonstrate that the power is necessary, that narrower alternatives are inadequate, that innocent people will not be broadly cataloged, and that the capability cannot predictably expand beyond its stated purpose.

The greater the power, the greater the burden. The broader the collection, the stronger the justification required. The more innocent people affected, the more skeptical the public should become. The closer information comes to the machinery of coercion, the more strictly access must be limited.

Citizens are not obligated to surrender liberty merely because officials promise to exercise restraint.

Government is obligated to explain why it should possess the power at all.

## Especially When It Craves It

There will always be reasons to expand surveillance. There will always be another crime that might have been solved faster, another suspect who might have been located sooner, another emergency in which additional information might have helped, another agency that could use the data, and another analytical tool promising greater insight.

The appetite for information is limitless because the possible uses of information are limitless.

That is precisely why the authority to collect it must not be limitless.

The state’s monopoly on violence makes its demands for surveillance uniquely consequential. No other institution can transform a database query into a compelled roadside encounter with the same legal authority. No other institution can so readily convert recorded movement into detention, interrogation, search, prosecution, and punishment.

This unique relationship requires unique restraint.

The institution with the greatest power to act on information should not possess the broadest power to collect it.

It should possess the narrowest.

Its authority should be defined by specific purposes, particularized suspicion, identifiable evidence, judicial process, necessity, and clear limits. Where those safeguards cannot make a surveillance system compatible with a free society, the system should not be deployed.

There are moments when government will insist that it needs broad powers. There are moments when officials will warn that limitations are dangerous. There are moments when fear will make sweeping authority seem prudent.

Those are not the moments to abandon restraint.

Those are the moments when restraint becomes most important.

Power must be limited even when it craves expansion.

Especially when it craves expansion.

## Conclusion

The roadside camera is not an isolated object. It is the visible edge of a much larger institutional system.

Behind it stands a database. Behind the database stands an official with access. Behind the official stands a police department. Behind the police department stands the legal authority to detain, search, seize, arrest, and use force.

The camera appears harmless only when we mentally separate it from everything to which it is connected.

Once the relationship is restored, the issue becomes clear.

Government surveillance is not simply observation conducted by government. It is observation joined to the state’s monopoly on violence. That combination gives government surveillance a character no private observation can possess.

It can transform information into compulsion, a record into an investigation, an alert into a traffic stop, an error into an armed encounter, and yesterday’s innocent journey into tomorrow’s evidence.

A free society does not deny that government may sometimes need information to investigate genuine crimes. It demands that such power remain narrow, particular, justified, reviewable, and exceptional.

The state should not record everyone because it may someday need to find someone. It should not construct a history of innocent movement because historical movement might prove useful. It should not treat the population as a standing pool of investigatory data.

The monopoly on violence is already an extraordinary concentration of power. It must never be casually joined to an equally extraordinary power of observation.

A surveillance camera does not need to carry a weapon to become part of the machinery of coercion.

It only needs to report to those who do.
