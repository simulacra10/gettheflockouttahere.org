# The Camera Does Not Carry a Gun

## Surveillance and the State’s Monopoly on Violence

A roadside license-plate camera does not look threatening. It does not wear a uniform, display a badge, carry handcuffs, issue commands, or place anyone under arrest. It simply stands beside the road, silently photographing passing vehicles and transmitting what it observes into a database.

That apparent passivity is precisely what makes the modern surveillance state so easy to misunderstand.

When most people see a camera, they think about observation. They compare it to a security camera outside a store, a doorbell camera on a neighbor’s home, or a police officer seeing a license plate while driving through town. From that perspective, an automated license-plate reader may seem like little more than an efficient pair of eyes.

But a government surveillance camera is not merely a pair of eyes. It is an extension of an institution possessing powers no private citizen, corporation, church, neighborhood association, or civic organization can lawfully claim. It supplies information to an institution that can stop people, detain them, search them, seize their property, arrest them, imprison them, and use escalating physical force against them if they resist.

The camera does not carry a gun.

It reports to those who do.

That is the relationship we must understand if we are to understand the surveillance state.

## The Monopoly on Violence

The phrase “monopoly on violence” can sound abstract, academic, or deliberately provocative. The idea itself is straightforward.

Within a defined territory, the state claims a unique authority to determine when physical force may lawfully be used. It authorizes police officers to detain and arrest, courts to impose punishment, and officials to seize property. It establishes the conditions under which its agents may use force, including deadly force.

Individuals may sometimes use force in self-defense, but they do so under rules ultimately interpreted and enforced by the state. A private citizen cannot create a police department, declare jurisdiction over the town, compel obedience, levy taxes, operate a jail, and issue final judgments in disputes involving himself.

The state claims that authority for itself.

Murray Rothbard described the state as “that organization in society which attempts to maintain a monopoly of the use of force and violence in a given territorial area.” He emphasized that other institutions ordinarily acquire their resources through voluntary exchange, while the state ultimately relies upon coercion.[^rothbard-anatomy]

Ludwig von Mises described the state as “the social apparatus of coercion and compulsion.”[^mises-market] Elsewhere, he wrote that government “has the monopoly of violent action.”[^mises-society]

The defining characteristic of government is not that it employs people, owns buildings, maintains records, or purchases technology. Private institutions do all those things. Its defining characteristic is that its commands can be backed by legally authorized force.

A church may request a contribution. A business may offer a service. A social organization may expel a member. A newspaper may criticize someone. Government can issue a command.

Behind that command stands enforcement. Behind enforcement stands physical restraint. Behind resistance stands the possibility of escalating violence.

This does not mean every government action is wicked or that every government employee is malicious. It means that government possesses a relationship to force that no other institution can claim. That unique power demands unique restraint.

## When Observation Becomes Coercion

A private store may record a vehicle entering its parking lot. It may use that recording to investigate theft, enforce rules on its property, or determine whether a particular person entered the premises. But the store ordinarily cannot follow the driver onto a public road, activate emergency lights, compel the vehicle to stop, order the occupants to show their hands, search the vehicle, confiscate property, place the driver in handcuffs, or transport that person to a detention facility.

The police can.

A neighbor may see that you left home in the morning. A coworker may notice your car outside a restaurant. A gas station employee may see you purchase fuel. Those observations are limited, dispersed, temporary, and generally disconnected from one another.

A government surveillance network can collect those observations automatically, consolidate them, preserve them, search them, compare them, and use them to reconstruct a person’s movements. The distinction is not merely one of scale, although the scale is significant. It is the union of information and coercive authority.

**Camera. Database. Search. Police encounter. Command. Force.**

The force may never be used. Most encounters may end peacefully. Most officers may behave professionally. But coercive authority is always present. When an officer activates emergency lights behind a vehicle, the driver is not receiving a friendly request that may be ignored without consequence. When an officer orders someone out of a car, the person does not negotiate as an equal party in a voluntary exchange.

The design of surveillance systems conceals this relationship because the coercion does not occur at the point of collection. The consequences may occur miles away when an officer receives an alert, days later when an investigator searches a plate number, or months later when stored observations are used to construct a pattern of movement or association.

People see the camera but not the command it may generate. They see the database but not the traffic stop. They see the promise of efficiency but not the consequences of error.

An error in an advertising system may produce an irrelevant advertisement. An error in a police surveillance system may produce flashing lights, shouted commands, detention, search, or an armed encounter.

The government’s mistake becomes the citizen’s emergency.

A surveillance camera does not need to exercise force directly to become part of the machinery of force. It needs only to provide actionable intelligence to those authorized to exercise it.

## From Suspicion to Population Surveillance

Traditional law enforcement was theoretically organized around particularized suspicion. A crime occurred. Evidence was gathered. A suspect was identified. Investigators then sought additional information related to the offense.

The modern surveillance state reverses that sequence. It collects information about everyone first.

Government may have no suspect and no individualized grounds to believe that a particular person has done anything wrong. Nevertheless, the system records that person’s movements and preserves them for possible future use. Every passing vehicle becomes an entry. Every driver becomes searchable. Every journey becomes potentially relevant to an investigation that may not yet exist.

Instead of government demonstrating a reason to investigate someone before collecting information, government collects information so that it may later decide whom to investigate.

This is not merely a more efficient version of an officer seeing a plate while patrolling. Human observation is limited by time, place, memory, attention, and purpose. Automated surveillance removes those natural limits. The system sees continuously, remembers automatically, searches retrospectively, and connects observations across time and place.

It permits government to ask not only, “Where is the vehicle we are looking for?” but also, “Where has this vehicle been?” “Which vehicles appeared near it?” and “What pattern can be inferred from its movements?”

The result is not simply observation. It is institutional memory backed by coercive authority.

## Power Cannot Depend on Virtue

Defenses of government power often contain an unspoken premise: the people entrusted with the power will be virtuous.

The officer will be virtuous. The police chief will be virtuous. The prosecutor, administrator, auditor, council member, judge, and future officeholder will all use the authority only for its intended purpose. They will resist curiosity, convenience, pressure, anger, ambition, political fashion, and personal interest.

This assumption is not merely optimistic. It contradicts what we know about human beings.

Government agents are not drawn from a separate and more virtuous species. They are people. They possess loyalties, ambitions, prejudices, fears, frustrations, career incentives, personal relationships, and private interests just as everyone else does. Some are honorable. Some are not. Most are mixtures of good and bad motives, as human beings ordinarily are.

The decisive difference is that government agents possess something the rest of us do not: access to the state’s monopoly on violence.

A private person’s selfishness may cause an insult, a dishonest transaction, or a personal betrayal. A government agent’s selfishness, error, impatience, or prejudice can be joined to detention, seizure, prosecution, and force.

A free society cannot be designed around the expectation of universal virtue. It must be designed in recognition of ordinary human nature. Its safeguards must continue to operate when officials are impatient, self-interested, mistaken, politically motivated, or simply unexceptional.

The argument for limitation does not require us to believe that every officer will abuse every power. It requires only the recognition that no institution can guarantee the permanent virtue of everyone who will control that power in the future.

A virtuous police chief can retire. A conscientious administrator can be replaced. A careful council can lose an election. A principled prosecutor can be succeeded by an ambitious one.

Rights cannot depend on the continuing virtue of temporary officeholders.

Power must be judged not only by the character of the person holding it today, but by the capabilities it creates for every person who may hold it tomorrow. Surveillance architecture outlives the promises used to introduce it. Administrations change. Technology improves. Databases merge. Retention periods expand. Search tools become more sophisticated.

The hardware does not care who controls it. The database does not remember the reassuring speech delivered when it was approved. The system simply waits for the next person authorized to search it.

## From Convenience to Mission Creep

The surveillance state rarely presents itself as a surveillance state. It presents itself through exceptional cases: a stolen vehicle, a kidnapped child, a violent fugitive, a murder suspect, or a person in immediate danger.

These examples are powerful because the harms are real. People understandably want police to recover stolen property, rescue abducted children, and apprehend violent offenders. But the emotional force of an exceptional case cannot answer the institutional question.

The question is not whether information can ever help solve a serious crime. Of course it can. The question is whether helping in some serious cases justifies building a system that records everyone.

The assurance that surveillance will be used “only for serious crimes” also misunderstands how people act within institutions. Praxeology begins with the recognition that human beings act purposefully. Officials are no exception. They choose among available means according to their goals, costs, expected benefits, time constraints, and personal preferences.

When a surveillance search requires a warrant, written justification, supervisory approval, outside review, and carries the possibility of meaningful consequences, it imposes a real cost. That cost gives officials a reason to reserve the power for important cases.

When searching a person’s movements requires only a few keystrokes, the calculation changes. If approval is routine or nonexistent, audits are rare, and misuse carries little chance of detection or punishment, convenience will steadily displace restraint.

That does not require uniquely corrupt people. It requires ordinary people responding to incentives.

An investigator under pressure to close a case will use the available tool. An officer trying to resolve uncertainty quickly will choose the fastest source of information. A supervisor concerned with performance statistics may encourage more searches. An employee curious about a neighbor, acquaintance, critic, or former romantic partner may decide that the chance of being caught is remote.

When the immediate benefit of a search is available now and the possible cost is distant, uncertain, or trivial, expedient use becomes predictable. Rules written on paper must compete with immediate convenience.

Repeated use creates habit. Habit becomes standard procedure. Standard procedure becomes institutional expectation.

From there, the appetite expands. Police want more effective investigative tools. Prosecutors want stronger evidence. Administrators want better metrics. Regulators want more records. Intelligence agencies want more patterns. Each request can sound modest when considered alone.

The danger emerges from accumulation.

A camera installed for one purpose becomes part of a network. The network produces a database. The database acquires search tools. Search tools produce analytical capabilities. Those capabilities attract new users. New users request broader retention, greater sharing, and additional reasons for access.

At each stage, the immediate institutional benefit is concentrated while the privacy cost is dispersed across thousands of people. More data reduces uncertainty. Fewer restrictions save time. Broader sharing increases convenience. The harm may remain invisible for years.

That imbalance makes expansion predictable. No official needs to announce the construction of a total surveillance state. Each participant needs only to request one additional capability that makes his own work easier.

Government will nearly always desire more information because information makes coercive administration easier. But the convenience of those who exercise power is not the highest political good.

A free society should deliberately preserve areas of uncertainty for the state. Government should not know everything it could find useful. The state’s frustration with limits is often evidence that the limits are functioning.

## The Apparatus Serves Whoever Controls It

Henry Hazlitt described government and its police forces as an “apparatus of coercion.” The danger of such an apparatus is that people tend to judge it by the target they presently want it used against.[^hazlitt]

Once the machinery exists, people repeatedly discover new reasons to use it. Each person imagines it directed toward someone he considers dangerous, unlawful, immoral, or socially harmful. Because he approves of the immediate objective, he becomes less cautious about the power being created.

This is where the issue of illegal immigration becomes especially important.

Many people who distrust government surveillance in other contexts become willing to accept it when the immediate target is someone believed to be in the country unlawfully. They see the enforcement goal as legitimate and therefore treat the surveillance mechanism as justified.

That reaction is understandable, but it confuses the desirability of a particular enforcement outcome with the desirability of the power used to obtain it.

A camera capable of identifying and reconstructing the movements of an undocumented immigrant is equally capable of identifying and reconstructing the movements of a gun owner, church member, political organizer, business owner, protester, journalist, government critic, medical patient, or ordinary family.

The machine does not understand why it was installed. It has no loyalty to the people who supported its deployment. It does not distinguish between a purpose one person considers legitimate and a purpose that same person would consider abusive. It records plates, locations, times, and patterns according to the permissions granted to whoever controls it.

A person may support surveillance when it is used to enforce immigration law while opposing its use to identify vehicles visiting gun stores, churches, political meetings, or private homes. Another may support it against violent suspects while opposing its use to identify vehicles visiting clinics, protests, labor meetings, advocacy organizations, or religious gatherings.

The contradiction does not lie in holding different moral judgments about those activities. They are not necessarily equivalent. The contradiction lies in believing that the surveillance power will remain attached only to the purpose one approves.

It will not.

The apparatus is not limited by the political justification that secured its creation. Once installed, it becomes a general capability awaiting new rules, users, databases, and definitions of acceptable use.

The underlying error is always the same: people evaluate coercive machinery according to its present target rather than its permanent capability.

They ask, “Do I want the government to find these people?”

They do not ask, “Do I want the government to possess the power to find anyone in this manner?”

That is the question that must control the debate.

The temptation is always to support coercive machinery when it is aimed at someone else. One person accepts it for immigration enforcement, another for firearms enforcement, another for narcotics, political extremism, unpaid fines, minor warrants, vehicle repossessions, or code violations.

Each justification appears limited when considered alone. Together they construct a system that can eventually be used against nearly everyone.

The proper test of a government power is not whether we approve of its first target. It is whether we are prepared to entrust that power to officials we distrust, acting for purposes we oppose, against people we believe are innocent.

If the answer is no, then the power itself is the problem.

The apparatus is indifferent to ideology, motive, and allegiance. It serves whoever controls the credentials.

## You Have Nothing to Hide Yet

Freedom of movement means more than the absence of checkpoints. A person may remain technically free to travel while every journey becomes a searchable government record. Once people know that destinations and associations may be examined later, they begin asking not only whether an activity is lawful, but whether it may someday look suspicious.

That is not the mentality of a free people.

The most common defense of surveillance may also be the most shortsighted: “I have nothing to hide.” Its companion is equally familiar: “Don’t commit crimes and you have nothing to worry about.”

Both statements rest on an assumption that is rarely examined. The speaker assumes that the legal and political conditions under which he currently lives will remain substantially unchanged after the surveillance infrastructure is in place.

He has nothing to hide yet.

He is committing no crime yet.

That does not mean he secretly intends wrongdoing. It means that he is treating today’s laws, freedoms, enforcement priorities, and political authorities as permanent.

They are not.

Laws change. Definitions change. Administrative rules change. Conduct once lawful becomes prohibited. Exceptions narrow. Licenses become mandatory. Political movements gain and lose power. Emergencies are declared. New categories of suspicious behavior are created.

The surveillance system remains.

A person who says, “I have nothing to hide,” imagines government looking only for conduct he already considers criminal. He does not imagine a future government examining conduct he presently regards as ordinary, private, lawful, or protected.

He may own firearms, attend a particular church, educate his children at home, operate a cash business, protest government policy, receive controversial medical treatment, donate to an unpopular organization, or meet privately with political allies. Today, he may consider those activities entirely legitimate. Tomorrow’s officials may classify some of them differently.

The critical mistake is believing that surveillance merely enforces a fixed body of law. In reality, it creates a durable capability that can be redirected whenever the law, policy, or political definition of wrongdoing changes.

A database does not know which freedoms its original supporters expected to retain. It does not distinguish between a liberty and an offense. It records the same plate, time, location, and association either way. Only the government’s current rules determine how the information is interpreted, and those rules can change without replacing a single camera.

“Don’t commit crimes” is therefore not an adequate answer. It assumes that criminality is a permanent moral category rather than a legal classification written and revised by human institutions. History is filled with examples of governments criminalizing peaceful speech, religious practice, association, labor organizing, forbidden literature, resistance to segregation, and assistance to people whom the state sought to capture.

The fact that conduct has been declared illegal does not establish that the law is just, and obedience to every existing law is not the definition of liberty.

The danger of comprehensive surveillance is not confined to people who intend to violate good laws. It extends to people who may one day refuse to obey bad ones. It also extends to people who remain lawful but become politically inconvenient.

Surveillance need not prove a crime to exert power. It can identify associations, expose private relationships, reveal attendance at meetings, generate investigative leads, produce suspicion, or chill lawful conduct.

The person who says he has nothing to hide is usually imagining his present self before a government he does not presently fear. He should instead imagine his future self before a government he does not trust.

Would he still be comfortable with a searchable record of his movements if the officials controlling it opposed his beliefs? Would he accept the system if its definitions of extremism, public safety, contraband, misinformation, unlawful assembly, or prohibited association were written by people hostile to everything he values?

If not, then his confidence was never based on principle. It was based on temporary political comfort.

Privacy is not the refuge of criminals. It is the space in which lawful people retain the freedom to live, associate, worship, speak, organize, dissent, and change their minds without creating a permanent government record.

The question is not whether you have something to hide from the government you have today. The question is whether you are willing to give every government you may have tomorrow the ability to search the life you are living now.

## Regulation Cannot Erase Capability

When surveillance systems become controversial, officials often propose regulation rather than prohibition. They promise limited retention periods, logged searches, access controls, audits, disciplinary consequences, and restrictions to approved law-enforcement purposes.

These measures may reduce some forms of abuse, but they do not resolve the central problem. The state still possesses the capability.

Hans-Hermann Hoppe’s criticism of the state as ultimate judge exposes the structural weakness. Government buys the system, writes the access policies, performs the searches, reviews employee conduct, interprets disclosure obligations, and frequently determines whether its own use was lawful. Oversight may help, but the institution remains deeply involved in judging itself.

A retention period can be extended. An approved-purpose list can be broadened. An audit can become superficial. A sharing agreement can move information beyond the jurisdiction that collected it. A disciplinary policy can be weakly enforced. A search log may reveal misuse only after the person has already been watched.

Regulation asks government to restrain itself while preserving the power that requires restraint.

Frédéric Bastiat argued that collective force cannot legitimately acquire a moral right that individuals themselves do not possess. An ordinary person could not justly catalog the movements of an entire town because some residents might someday commit crimes. Conducting that activity through government does not erase the moral problem. It joins the collection to coercive power.[^bastiat]

Certain government functions necessarily involve sensitive information and require strict procedures, warrants, minimization, disclosure, outside review, and meaningful penalties. But not every possible government capability must be preserved merely so it can be regulated.

The burden should fall upon the state to prove not merely that a system is useful, but that the function cannot be performed through narrower means. Can police use targeted alerts tied to specifically identified vehicles? Can they seek judicial authorization during an investigation? Can they rely on witness statements, patrol work, dispatch information, reported sightings, and footage from locations relevant to a known crime?

The question should never be merely, “Could police find this useful?” Almost any information could be useful.

The proper question is, “Does that usefulness justify granting this capability to the institution possessing the monopoly on violence?”

That is a much higher standard. It should be.

## Especially When It Craves It

Surveillance debates are often framed as if citizens must prove that the technology will be abused. Show us the misconduct. Identify the victim. Prove that officials searched without justification.

That reverses the proper burden.

The public should not have to wait for abuse before questioning a sweeping power. The institution seeking the authority should have to demonstrate necessity, explain why narrower alternatives are inadequate, and show why innocent people must be broadly cataloged.

The greater the power, the greater the burden. The broader the collection, the stronger the justification required. The closer information comes to the machinery of coercion, the more strictly access must be limited.

There will always be reasons to expand surveillance: another crime that might have been solved faster, another suspect who might have been located sooner, another emergency in which more information might have helped, another agency that could use the data, and another analytical tool promising greater insight.

The appetite for information is limitless because the possible uses of information are limitless.

That is precisely why the authority to collect it must not be limitless.

No other institution can transform a database query into a compelled roadside encounter with the same legal authority. No other institution can so readily convert recorded movement into detention, interrogation, search, prosecution, and punishment.

This unique relationship requires unique restraint.

The institution with the greatest power to act on information should not possess the broadest power to collect it.

It should possess the narrowest.

Government surveillance authority should be particular, justified, reviewable, and exceptional. Where those safeguards cannot make a system compatible with a free society, the system should not be deployed.

The roadside camera is not an isolated object. Behind it stands a database. Behind the database stands an official with access. Behind the official stands a police department. Behind the police department stands the legal authority to detain, search, seize, arrest, and use force.

The camera appears harmless only when we mentally separate it from everything to which it is connected. Once the relationship is restored, the issue becomes clear.

The monopoly on violence is already an extraordinary concentration of power. It must never be casually joined to an equally extraordinary power of observation.

Power must be limited even when it craves expansion.

Especially when it craves expansion.

A surveillance camera does not need to carry a weapon to become part of the machinery of coercion.

It only needs to report to those who do.
